Discovery — Matching

The Journey — We begin in continental Hungary with a bone-dry Furmint from Tokaj's oldest classified vineyard — all mineral tension and electric acidity, designed to sharpen the palate before the richness that follows. From there we move to the volcanic slopes of Soave: Pieropan's Calvarino adds textural weight and almond complexity — still crisp, but broader, warming you into the meal. The Patrimonio Vermentino from Corsica lifts us again — Mediterranean generosity with a limestone-driven saline backbone, bridging the whites toward something more substantial. Then the shift: Chiarlo's Barbaresco Reyna introduces Nebbiolo's aristocratic tannins and floral perfume — rose petals and red cherry after the minerality of the whites — a deliberate contrast that resets the palate for the final wine. We close with Justino's Fine Rich Madeira — fortified, medium-sweet, oxidative — a complete departure that moves us from the savoury world into something warm, caramelised and persistent. The progression runs from taut and mineral → textured and saline → structured and tannic → sweet and oxidative. Each wine exists to make the next feel inevitable.

Mandolás, Oremus, Furmint, Tokaj, Hungary 2023
ProducerTokaj-Oremus (Tempos Vega Sicilia)
AppellationTokaji
Region, CountryTokaj, Hungary
Vintage2023
Alcohol~13 % vol.
StyleDry white
Grape VarietiesFurmint (100%)

The Producer

Oremus belongs to the Álvarez family, the same people behind Vega Sicilia in Ribera del Duero — arguably Spain's most revered wine estate. They acquired Oremus in 1993, just three years after the fall of communism in Hungary, when Tokaj's centuries-old reputation was intact but its vineyards were in a state of post-Soviet neglect. The family invested heavily, modernising the winery while respecting the traditions that made Tokaj famous in the first place.

But the history here goes far deeper than the Álvarez era. The Oremus vineyard was classified as Primae Classis (First Growth) in the world's very first official vineyard classification, established in 1772 — predating Bordeaux's 1855 classification by nearly a century. In 1650, wines from this specific vineyard were presented as a gift to Pope Benedict XIV. The name "Oremus" (Latin for "Let us pray") comes from the original ecclesiastical owners.

While the world knows Tokaj for its legendary sweet Aszú wines, Mandolás represents the dry side of Furmint: a grape of extraordinary acidity, mineral tension and terroir transparency that is increasingly compared to Riesling and Chablis. This is the estate's flagship dry white, the wine that proves Furmint deserves to be considered among Europe's great white grape varieties.

Viticulture

The vineyards sit in a continental climate where extreme seasonal variation — cold winters, warm summers and long, misty autumns — develops unrivalled aromatic complexity in the grapes. The estate farms around 115 hectares across some of Tokaj's finest classified vineyards. For the Mandolás, only healthy, non-botrytised fruit is selected, allowing the wine to express the pure varietal character of Furmint without the influence of noble rot. The volcanic soils contribute a mineral intensity and smoky character to the wines.

Winemaking

The grapes are gently pressed and fermented in a combination of stainless steel tanks and Hungarian oak barrels. The wine is aged on its fine lees for several months, building textural depth and complexity while preserving the grape's natural high acidity. The oak influence is subtle — present for structure and micro-oxygenation rather than flavour — allowing the varietal character and terroir to dominate.

Tasting Notes

A gastronomic Furmint with subtle aromas of lime, green apple, white pear, citrus peel and white flowers, underpinned by a saline, mineral quality. On the palate it is pure and taut, with bright, almost electric acidity and a medium-bodied structure that feels lean and precise. The finish is limpid and transparent, with a chalky mineral persistence that lingers. Drinking well now but with the architecture to age and develop further complexity.

Vintage 2023

The 2023 growing season in Tokaj was marked by a cooler spring followed by a warm, dry summer that maintained excellent sanitary conditions in the vineyard. The continental climate's signature extreme day-night temperature variation was particularly pronounced during September, preserving high natural acidity in the Furmint while allowing full phenolic ripeness. Harvest was slightly later than in recent warm vintages, resulting in wines with remarkable freshness, crystalline fruit definition and lower alcohol levels than the preceding two years. For dry Furmint specifically, 2023 is being described as a vintage of precision and minerality.

Soave Classico "Calvarino", Pieropan, Veneto, Italy 2023
ProducerPieropan
AppellationSoave Classico DOC
Region, CountryVeneto, Italy
Vintage2023
Alcohol~12.5 % vol.
StyleDry white
Grape VarietiesGarganega (70%), Trebbiano di Soave (30%)

The Producer

Pieropan is the family that single-handedly rescued Soave's reputation. In a world where Soave had become synonymous with cheap, industrial white wine, Leonildo Pieropan was the first to bottle a single-vineyard Soave, releasing the inaugural Calvarino in 1971. It was a radical act: proving that this appellation, on volcanic soils with ancient vines, could produce wines of genuine complexity and longevity.

Now in its fourth generation, the estate remains a reference point for what Italian white wine can be. The Calvarino vineyard (whose name means "little Calvary," a reference to the steep, punishing terrain) is the spiritual heart of the domaine. It sits alongside La Rocca as one of two single-vineyard wines that have earned Pieropan distinction far beyond the peninsula. All farming is now certified organic.

Viticulture

The Calvarino vineyard sits at approximately 250 metres above sea level within the Colombara UGA of the Soave Classico zone. The soils are volcanic basalt with limestone components, a geological profile that imparts distinctive mineral tension and depth. The vineyard is south-facing with steep slopes that Leonildo Pieropan first planted over 60 years ago. The combination of altitude, volcanic soils and old vines creates a wine of a completely different register from generic Soave.

Winemaking

Grapes are hand-harvested and undergo gentle whole-cluster pressing. The must is fermented and aged in tulip-shaped, glass-lined concrete tanks, which provide a neutral environment that preserves varietal character while allowing slow, gentle micro-oxygenation. The wine rests on its fine lees for approximately 12 months, building complexity and textural depth before bottling. No oak is used.

The Blend: What Each Grape Brings

Garganega (70%) — Late-ripening, vigorous, thick-skinned. Registered in the Veneto since the 13th century. Loosely clustered berries of pale white-green colour with moderate natural acidity. On volcanic basalt at Classico altitudes (200–350m), restricted yields concentrate the grape's characteristic almond kernel, white peach and citrus-blossom aromatics. The thick skins provide phenolic richness and textural weight — the silky, slightly oily mouthfeel that distinguishes great Soave from generic Italian whites. Garganega ages remarkably: over 5–15 years it develops honeyed, waxy, lanolin-like complexity. DNA profiling shows it is parent to Trebbiano Toscano (Ugni Blanc), making it one of Italy's most genetically significant varieties.

Trebbiano di Soave (30%) — Confirmed by DNA analysis (SSR markers) to be genetically identical to Verdicchio of the Marche (Italian Wine Central, University of Milan studies). Not related to Trebbiano Toscano despite the shared name. Trebbiano di Soave/Verdicchio is higher in natural acidity and more aromatically complex than Garganega — it contributes the green-apple crunch, citrus-peel freshness and saline minerality that sharpens and lifts the blend. In the Calvarino, its role is structural: providing the acidity backbone and mineral tension that give the wine its ageing architecture. Without it, Garganega alone would produce a rounder, softer wine with less precision and shorter cellaring potential. Research (MDPI, 2019) has identified high levels of methyl salicylate glycosides in Trebbiano di Soave — a compound linked to herbal, wintergreen aromatics that contribute to the grape's distinctive aromatic complexity.

Tasting Notes

Pale straw yellow with greenish reflections when young. The nose is beautifully complex, with aromas of white spring wildflower, honey, citrus (lemon zest), wet flint, Alpine herbs and stone fruit (apricot, poached apple). On the palate the wine shows textural heft and a silky approach, wrapping smoothly across the palate with flavours of green apple, almond, blossom and oyster shell. The finish is chalky and persistent, driven by a crystalline mineral freshness.

Vintage 2023

2023 in the Veneto was erratic and atypical. Cooler temperatures in April and May eventually gave way to heat, but rain persisted throughout much of the growing season, reaching an unparalleled peak in July, particularly in the Classico zone. The month also brought localised hailstorms of varying intensity. However, the cooler temperatures ultimately benefited Garganega and Trebbiano, which need time to develop their complex aromatics. The slightly late harvest, combined with the volcanic soils' excellent drainage, meant that producers on the best sites (like Pieropan's elevated Calvarino) came through the difficult year with concentrated, focused wines while lower-lying vineyards struggled.

Patrimonio, Domaine Paradella, Vermentino, Corsica, France 2022 — Magnum
ProducerDomaine Paradella
AppellationAOC Patrimonio
Region, CountryCorsica, France
Vintage2022
FormatMagnum (150cl)
Alcohol~13 % vol.
StyleDry white
Grape VarietiesVermentino (100%)

The Producer

Domaine Paradella is a young estate that produces wines with levels of elegance and sophistication that fly in the face of any notions of rusticity in Corsican wines. Lionel Wojcik founded the domaine in 2016, but his pedigree is anything but young: trained in Bordeaux, he served his apprenticeship under Louis Mitjavile at Château Le Tertre Rotebœuf in Saint-Émilion, one of the Right Bank's most legendary estates, famous for velvety, deeply concentrated wines that transcend their modest appellation. That Bordelais rigour is now transplanted to the Mediterranean.

In 2015, Wojcik returned to his native Corsica and took over 8 hectares of vines in Patrimonio. He uprooted the existing Carignan and replanted exclusively with indigenous varieties — Nielluccio and Vermentinu — committing entirely to the island's identity. The estate works organically on clay-limestone soils swept by sea breezes, using indigenous yeasts and minimal intervention in the cellar. Patrimonio is Corsica's oldest and most prestigious wine appellation, established in 1968 as the island's first AOC.

Viticulture

Patrimonio is arguably the most geologically complex wine region on Corsica: a mix of limestone, schist and granite — three of the greatest bedrocks for wine growing — with clay, silt, sand and gravel on the surface. Paradella's Vermentinu vines grow on clay-limestone, the soil type that gives Patrimonio whites their distinctive structure and mineral complexity. The Mediterranean climate is tempered by maritime influence and proximity to the Étang de Biguglia, a coastal lagoon that creates a microclimate preserving natural acidity. Warm days ripen the fruit generously while sea breezes and cool nights maintain freshness.

Winemaking

Hand-harvested grapes are gently pressed. Fermentation is spontaneous, using only indigenous yeasts, reflecting the specific microbial fingerprint of the vineyard. The winemaking is deliberately low-intervention, prioritising terroir expression and the grape's natural aromatic profile. The Magnum format provides a slower, more graceful evolution during ageing, resulting in greater complexity and freshness at the point of drinking.

Tasting Notes

Pale gold with intense brilliance. The nose presents complex aromas of white blossom, hawthorn, honey, citrus fruit and subtle notes of vanilla and toasted bread. On the palate it is fresh, mineral and suave — ample and racy with beautiful roundness and concentration. It unfolds slowly into a long, slightly smoky finish with notes of lime blossom and violet. A saline mineral backbone reflects the limestone terroir. This is not simple southern white wine; it is one of Corsica's most compelling expressions of Vermentino.

Vintage 2022

The 2022 vintage in Corsica was exceptionally dry, with harvests in Haute-Corse (northern Corsica, where Patrimonio is located) beginning at the start of August, one to three weeks earlier than usual. The dryness brought concentration to the wines, and the clay-limestone soils of Patrimonio, which retain moisture better than the granite of other Corsican appellations, helped buffer the vines against the worst effects of drought. The resulting whites show good concentration and structure from the small, thick-skinned berries, with the extended warmth of the vintage lending generosity to the mid-palate.

Barbaresco "Reyna", Michele Chiarlo, Piedmont, Italy 2022
ProducerMichele Chiarlo
AppellationBarbaresco DOCG
Region, CountryPiedmont, Italy
Vintage2022
Alcohol~14 % vol.
StyleDry red
Grape VarietiesNebbiolo (100%)

The Producer

Michele Chiarlo was one of the architects of modern Piedmont. Born into a winegrowing family in Calamandrana, he studied oenology at the wine school in Alba alongside Giacomo Tachis (future creator of Sassicaia and Tignanello) and Renato Ratti (who would revolutionise Barolo). He established his winery in 1956 and bottled his first Barolo in 1958. In 1974, he pioneered the use of malolactic fermentation for Barbera, a technical advance that transformed the grape from a harsh, acidic workhorse into something elegant and age-worthy.

Michele passed away in 2023, but his sons Alberto and Stefano continue his vision across approximately 150 hectares in the finest crus of Barolo, Barbaresco, Nizza and Gavi. They are one of only a handful of producers with significant holdings in both Barolo and Barbaresco, allowing them to showcase the difference between the "King" and the "Queen" of Italian wine.

The name "Reyna" means "Queen," a deliberate reference to Barbaresco's traditional title. Where Barolo is power, structure and austerity, Barbaresco is grace, perfume and elegance. This wine is their expression of that feminine ideal: aristocratic Nebbiolo from two of the DOCG's finest communes.

Viticulture

The vineyards of Reyna lie on steep hillsides in the communes of Barbaresco and Neive, facing southeast and southwest. The soils are bluish-grey calcareous clay marl (Tortonian marl), rich in microelements that lend complexity and mineral depth, with more sand in the Barbaresco plots adding aromatic finesse. Only in these choicest positions within the three permitted communes (Barbaresco, Treiso and Neive) can Nebbiolo be planted for Barbaresco production. Yields are naturally low on these hillside sites.

Winemaking

Grapes are hand-harvested and undergo a prolonged maceration on skins in temperature-controlled stainless steel tanks. The extraction is relatively gentle, seeking fine-grained tannins and aromatic complexity rather than brute power. Following fermentation, the wine is aged in a combination of large Slavonian oak botti and smaller French oak barrels, allowing the Nebbiolo's natural elegance to develop while building structural complexity. Total ageing meets the DOCG minimum of 26 months, with additional time in bottle before release.

Tasting Notes

Garnet red with brilliant orange reflections at the rim. The nose is classically floral — rose petals dominate, joined by red cherry, tea leaves, balsamic notes, orange peel and wild herbs. On the palate it is medium-bodied with crisp acidity and excellent fruit definition, supported by firm, dusty yet ripe tannins that are aristocratic rather than aggressive. The finish is long and full of flavour, with spice and dried herb complexity developing as the wine breathes. Still young and will reward patience — best from 2026 onwards.

Vintage 2022

Piedmont's 2022 was one of the warmest and driest vintages on record, echoing the broader European pattern of that year. Rain was scarce from spring through late summer, creating significant hydric stress in many vineyards. However, Barbaresco's Tortonian marl soils — heavier and more water-retentive than Barolo's sandier terrain — provided a crucial buffer. The heat pushed Nebbiolo to full phenolic ripeness earlier than usual, with many producers harvesting in early-to-mid October rather than the traditional late October. The resulting wines are deeply coloured and concentrated, with ripe tannins and generous fruit, yet the best (from cooler exposures and older vines) retain the freshness and elegance that distinguishes Barbaresco from its more powerful neighbour. Critics have described 2022 Barbarescos as "approachable and generous in youth" — wines that will drink well relatively early while still having the structure for medium-term development.

Justino's, Fine Rich Reserve, Madeira, Portugal 5Y
ProducerJustino's Madeira Wines (Vinhos Justino Henriques)
AppellationDOC Madeirense
Region, CountryMadeira Island, Portugal
VintageNV (5 Year Reserve)
Alcohol~19 % vol.
StyleMadeira (medium-rich, fortified)
Grape VarietiesTinta Negra

The Producer

Justino Henrique Freitas founded his house in 1870 on an island that had been exporting wine since the fifteenth century — when ships bound for the Indies would stop in Funchal to load barrels that would be accidentally "cooked" in the hold during months at sea. Those wines arrived at their destination tasting better than when they left. By the time Justino's was established, Madeira had perfected the art of replicating that oceanic ageing on land — and the house has been doing it for over 150 years.

Today, Justino's is believed to be Madeira's largest shipper. But size does not mean compromise here. In a region where the most celebrated houses — Blandy's, Henriques & Henriques, Barbeito — produce tiny quantities of single-variety wines at eye-watering prices, Justino's fills a vital role: making serious, well-crafted Madeira that introduces people to the style without requiring the commitment of a 20-year-old Bual or Malmsey. Their reserves and colheitas consistently punch above their weight, offering a window into Madeira's extraordinary character at a fraction of the cost of the noble-variety wines.

The "Fine Rich" designation indicates a medium-sweet style — sweeter than "Dry" or "Medium Dry," lighter than "Sweet." It is the most approachable entry point into Madeira's world. But even at this level, you taste the hallmarks that make Madeira unique among fortified wines: the warmth from fortification, the caramelised depth from controlled oxidation, and crucially, the searing acidity that prevents the sweetness from ever feeling heavy. That acidity is Madeira's secret weapon. It is what gives even a five-year-old wine a freshness that no other fortified style can match.

The Island & Viticulture

Madeira is a volcanic island 600 kilometres off the coast of Morocco — subtropical, steep, and spectacularly unsuited to conventional viticulture. Vineyards are planted on terraces carved into near-vertical slopes (poios), most below 300 metres elevation, in volcanic basalt soils enriched with organic matter. The maritime climate is warm and humid, with cooling trade winds from the northeast. Tinta Negra — the workhorse variety that accounts for roughly 85% of all Madeira production — thrives in these conditions. It is remarkably versatile: depending on where it is grown and how the wine is made, it can approximate the character of all four noble varieties (Sercial, Verdelho, Bual, Malmsey), from bone-dry to lusciously sweet.

The Grape: Tinta Negra

Tinta Negra (formerly called Tinta Negra Mole) is a red-skinned grape of uncertain parentage, possibly a crossing of Pinot Noir and Grenache. Despite its dominance in production, it has only recently begun to receive the respect it deserves — for decades it was not permitted to appear on labels, hidden behind terms like "Fine" or "Reserve." Since 2015, EU regulations have allowed Tinta Negra to be named on the label, a belated recognition of its quality. In the "Rich" style, it delivers aromas of dried fruit, caramel and toasted nuts, with a rounder, sweeter profile than the noble varieties Sercial or Verdelho, but more lifted and fresh than Malmsey. Its natural high acidity is the key to its versatility across all sweetness levels.

Winemaking & Ageing

After fermentation on the island, the wine is fortified with grape spirit (aguardente) to approximately 19% alcohol — the point of fortification determines sweetness (earlier = sweeter, as fermentation is arrested with residual sugar). The wine then undergoes the process that defines Madeira: controlled heating. For the 5 Year Reserve, this may involve estufagem (heating in concrete or stainless steel tanks at 45–50°C for a minimum of three months) or, for higher-quality lots, canteiro (natural warming in heated lofts over several years, where barrels are stored in warm upper floors of the lodge). The minimum ageing is five years in cask before bottling. This controlled oxidation and maderisation develops the characteristic burnt-caramel, nutty, dried-fruit flavours while simultaneously making the wine essentially indestructible: once opened, Madeira lasts months — even years — without deteriorating, because it has already undergone the very processes (heat and oxygen) that destroy other wines.

Tasting Notes

Amber-tawny colour with orange-gold reflections. The nose is immediately warming: dried figs, caramel, toffee, toasted walnuts, marmalade and a subtle smokiness. Medium-sweet on the palate — rich and rounded, with flavours of raisin, burnt sugar, orange peel and gentle baking spice — but the hallmark Madeira acidity is right there, cutting through the sweetness with a freshness that lifts the entire profile. Clean, warming finish with a persistence that belies its youth. At five years, this is Madeira in its most approachable guise, yet already showing the complexity and balance that distinguishes the island's wines from every other fortified style on earth.

Service & Pairing

Serve slightly cool (14–16°C). Works beautifully with chocolate desserts, blue cheese (Stilton, Roquefort), crème brûlée, pecan pie, or simply on its own as a digestif. The "Rich" sweetness level also bridges savoury-sweet: excellent with foie gras or smoked duck. Once opened, keep indefinitely — Madeira does not deteriorate.

Sources: Justino's Madeira (official) · Madeira Wine & Dine · Taste Atlas (canteiro method)

Prestige — Matching

The Journey — We open on volcanic rock: Assyrtiko from Santorini — bone-dry, searingly mineral, with the salinity of the Aegean still in the glass. It is a deliberate provocation: acidity and minerality at their most extreme, a palate-cleanser that prepares you for everything that follows. The second wine, La Soufrandière's Pouilly-Vinzelles, shifts us to Burgundian limestone — still mineral, still precise, but warmer, rounder, with the generous texture of biodynamic Chardonnay on fine lees. A step from volcanic austerity toward earthy richness. Then Pico: Tarelo's Arinto/Verdelho blend returns us to the Atlantic — volcanic again, but this time with the textural generosity of Verdelho softening the mineral spine. Three volcanic/mineral whites in sequence, but each completely different in geography, grape and texture — Santorini (pumice, no soil), Burgundy (Jurassic limestone), Azores (basalt lava). The fourth wine breaks the pattern entirely: Château Bauduc's Bordeaux rouge introduces tannin and dark fruit — Merlot's supple plum against Cabernet's cassis structure. After three taut whites, the red feels generous and grounding. We close with Nicolas Réau's Coteaux de l'Aubance — Chenin Blanc, sweet, botrytised, electric with acidity — a dessert wine that refuses to be heavy. The progression: mineral tension → limestone warmth → Atlantic texture → tannic structure → sweet precision.

The Producer

Gaia Wines was founded in 1994 by Leon Karatsalos, a viticulturist, and Yiannis Paraskevopoulos, a Bordeaux-trained oenologist with a PhD in winemaking. Their mission was to prove that Greece's indigenous grapes could produce wines of genuine world-class stature. The estate is named after "Mother Earth," honouring the unique terroir that gives birth to their wines. They operate two wineries: one on the volcanic island of Santorini and another in Nemea on the mainland.

On Santorini, the vines are ancient and ungrafted — some approaching 80 years old — grown using the traditional "kouloura" method, where each plant is woven into a low basket shape to protect the grapes from the relentless Aegean wind and scorching sun. There is no irrigation possible on Santorini; the vines survive on morning sea mist alone. It is viticulture at its most extreme, and the wines it produces are unlike anything else: bone-dry, searingly mineral, with an electric acidity that comes from volcanic pumice rather than clay or limestone.

Viticulture

The vineyards are located in the Episkopi, Akrotiri and Pyrgos regions of Santorini. The soils are purely volcanic: pumice,ite and ash deposited by the cataclysmic eruption of 1600 BC. There is essentially no organic matter. The vines, ungrafted and pre-phylloxera (the pest cannot survive in volcanic sand), draw their character directly from this mineral-rich terrain. The kouloura basket-training protects against 70 km/h winds and reflects heat away from the fruit. Yields are naturally tiny.

Winemaking

Grapes are hand-harvested in the early morning hours to preserve freshness. Gentle pressing is followed by temperature-controlled fermentation in stainless steel to capture the pure varietal and terroir character. The wine is kept on its fine lees for several months, building texture without obscuring the mineral core. No oak is used.

Tasting Notes

Aromas of lemon zest, green apple, sea spray and crushed stone. The palate is taut and bone-dry, with electric acidity and a long, bracingly salty finish. There is a smoky, flinty minerality that speaks directly of the volcanic soil. Vibrant citrus and a clean mineral backbone create a crisp, well-balanced medium-bodied mouthfeel. This wine will continue to evolve for two to five years, as the mineral notes soften and more nuanced flavours of fruit and honey develop.

Vintage 2024

Santorini's 2024 growing season was characterised by a mild spring and a warm, dry summer — conditions that favour Assyrtiko's signature acidity retention. The absence of rain through the ripening period (typical for the island) concentrated flavours in the small, thick-skinned berries. Cool maritime winds from the Aegean moderated daytime temperatures, preserving the freshness that defines the best Santorini whites. Early reports indicate wines of exceptional mineral tension and citrus purity.

The Producer

Domaine La Soufrandière is the passion project of brothers Jean-Philippe and Jean-Guillaume Bret, who grew up in the outskirts of Paris but spent every holiday with their grandparents in Vinzelles. Their grandfather Jules Bret purchased the property in 1947, but for decades the vineyards were under a sharecropping agreement, with all fruit going to the local cooperative. In 2000, the brothers took the vineyards back and began farming them themselves. By 2006, they had achieved full Demeter biodynamic certification.

They now work approximately 12 hectares in Pouilly-Vinzelles and surrounding appellations, supplemented by a small high-quality négoce operation under the "Bret Brothers" label. Their philosophy is simple: "The love of nature, the passion for Burgundy's great terroirs, and sharing over a glass of living, vibrant wine." In a region where the southern Mâconnais is often dismissed as Burgundy's poor cousin, the Bret brothers are proving that these clay-limestone hillsides can produce Chardonnays of real depth, complexity and longevity.

"Les Longeays" is one of the great individual climats (named vineyard sites) within Pouilly-Vinzelles. It scored 94/100 in La Revue du Vin de France, which praised its "impressive intensity and dry extract, its solar energy, and its dynamic pulsation." This is biodynamic Burgundy from one of the Mâconnais's most serious estates.

Viticulture

The Les Longeays climat sits on argilo-calcaire (clay-limestone) soils typical of the best Mâconnais sites, with a south-east exposure on mid-slope. The vines are farmed biodynamically (Demeter-certified since 2006), with preparations applied according to the lunar calendar and no synthetic inputs. The geological faults running through the Mâconnais have created a patchwork of distinct terroirs, and Les Longeays benefits from its specific combination of limestone bedrock and clay overlay.

Winemaking

Hand-harvested grapes undergo whole-cluster pressing. Fermentation begins with indigenous yeasts in a combination of used oak barrels and concrete tanks. The wine is aged on its fine lees for approximately 12 months, building textural complexity while preserving the energy and mineral tension of the site. The oak is deliberately neutral, serving as a vessel rather than a flavouring agent.

Tasting Notes

Citrus blossom, ripe pear and crushed stone shine through, with hints of honey and saline minerality adding depth. The palate shows crunchy, crystalline limestone character — intensely citrus-driven with impressive extract and energy. Lively acidity and a layered mid-palate create a long, elegant finish. There is a solar richness to the wine balanced by its dynamic, pulsating freshness. A refined and expressive single-vineyard Burgundy.

Vintage 2022

The 2022 vintage in the Mâconnais was warm and dry, part of the broader European pattern that year. However, the southern Burgundy vineyards on clay-limestone handled the conditions better than many: the clay retained moisture through the driest periods, while the limestone provided freshness and mineral tension. The resulting whites are generous and solar, with ripe fruit concentration balanced by the natural acidity that the best sites preserve. La Revue du Vin de France described the 2022 Mâconnais as "rich but pulsating with energy" — a vintage where the warmth added weight without sacrificing drinkability.

The Producer

Winemaking on Pico is one of the most extraordinary stories in European viticulture. The island, dominated by Portugal's highest mountain (2,351 metres), produces wine from vines planted directly in volcanic basalt — black lava rock with virtually no soil. The landscape is so remarkable that the UNESCO World Heritage committee designated Pico's vineyard culture a protected site in 2004, recognising the "currais" — hand-built stone walls of basalt that shelter each individual vine from the Atlantic winds — as a masterwork of human adaptation to a hostile environment.

Tarelo works within this extraordinary terroir, producing wines from two of the island's most characterful indigenous varieties: Arinto dos Açores and Verdelho. Both grapes are uniquely adapted to Pico's volcanic conditions and maritime climate, producing wines of remarkable mineral intensity, salinity and freshness. Production on Pico is inherently tiny — the heroic viticulture required to farm these lava fields means yields are minuscule and every bottle represents an extraordinary amount of human labour.

Viticulture

The vines grow in cracks and pockets within pure volcanic basalt lava rock — the biscoitos — protected from Atlantic winds by thousands of hand-built stone walls (currais) that create a network of micro-vineyards across the landscape. There is effectively no soil in the conventional sense: just decomposed volcanic rock, pumice and ash. This extreme terroir, combined with the maritime climate's constant humidity and moderate temperatures, produces grapes of extraordinary mineral concentration. The volcanic rock absorbs heat during the day and releases it slowly at night. No irrigation is possible; the vines rely entirely on rainfall and sea moisture.

Winemaking

The winemaking reflects the terroir's purity: gentle pressing, temperature-controlled fermentation to preserve the delicate aromatics and mineral character. The wine is aged in a combination of stainless steel and neutral vessels to maintain focus on varietal expression and volcanic terroir.

The Blend: What Each Grape Brings

Arinto dos Açores — Genetically distinct from mainland Arinto (Arinto de Bucelas/Pedernã), despite sharing the name. António Maçanita (Azores Wine Company) confirms: "Although it shares the same name and good acidity of the mainland Arinto, the grapes are not related." Native to the Azores, uniquely adapted to volcanic basalt soils and high-humidity Atlantic conditions. Characterised by very high natural acidity (comparable to Riesling levels — 7–9 g/L total), lean body, and pronounced mineral/saline character. On Pico's basalt, it develops iodine, crushed-stone and smoky-flinty aromatics directly derived from the volcanic terroir. In the blend, Arinto dos Açores provides the structural skeleton: searing acidity, salinity and a long, taut mineral finish. It is the vertical, architectural component.

Verdelho — One of Madeira's four noble grape varieties and historically the dominant variety on Pico. Introduced to the Azores by Franciscan friars in the 15th century (reportedly from Sicilian stock — "Verdecchio siciliano"). Verdelho wine from Pico was exported throughout northern Europe and Russia — cases were found in the Czar's wine cellars after 1917. The grape is characterised by moderate-to-high acidity, higher alcohol potential than Arinto, and an aromatic richness spanning citrus, tropical fruit (guava, passionfruit), white flowers and honey. On Pico's lava rock, Verdelho contributes textural generosity, aromatic complexity and a perfumed mid-palate richness that balances Arinto's austerity. Where Arinto is angular and mineral, Verdelho is rounder and more aromatic — the blend's tension derives from this contrast between structure and flesh.

Tasting Notes

A textured, mineral white with pronounced volcanic character. The nose is intense: citrus peel, sea salt, crushed stone, and subtle smoky notes from the basalt terroir. On the palate it is crisp and taut, with a rich mid-palate texture balanced by bright, driving acidity and a distinctive saline, almost iodine-like finish. The wine has depth and concentration despite its modest alcohol, with flavours of spiced pear, white flowers and a lingering mineral persistence. A wine shaped entirely by its place — one of the most extreme and beautiful in European viticulture.

Vintage 2019

The 2019 growing season on Pico was characterised by mild temperatures and good rainfall distribution through spring and early summer, followed by a dry, warm ripening period through August and September. These are ideal conditions for the island's indigenous white varieties, which need time to develop their complex aromatics while retaining the electric acidity that defines Azorean wines. The resulting wines show both concentration and freshness, with the volcanic mineral character at its most expressive.

The Producer

In 1999, Gavin Quinney sold his shares in a London computer company, sold the family house in southwest London, packed up two small children, and moved to a 60-acre vineyard called Château Bauduc near Créon in Bordeaux's Entre-Deux-Mers. His prior wine experience consisted entirely of drinking the stuff. Within five years, his white Bordeaux was the house wine at Restaurant Gordon Ramsay in Chelsea — a three-Michelin-star establishment — and his red was appearing at Rick Stein's in Padstow.

That a self-taught British winemaker could, within a few years, produce wine good enough for one of the world's finest restaurant wine lists says something about Bordeaux's Entre-Deux-Mers: these are vineyards with genuine terroir, consistently overlooked because they lack the prestige appellations of the Left and Right Banks. Quinney became Jancis Robinson's go-to correspondent for annual Bordeaux harvest reports — his detailed weather analyses appear on her website year after year, trusted by one of wine's sharpest critics.

The "Restaurant Collection" is the red cuvée selected specifically for Gordon Ramsay's restaurants. It is not a vanity project or a celebrity label in the modern sense: Quinney makes the wine, Ramsay's team selects it because it works with their food. Minimal intervention, native yeast fermentation, no fining, no filtration, responsibly minimal sulphur. The philosophy is straightforward: a balanced, food-friendly Bordeaux that drinks well immediately without needing years of cellaring — always 100% vegan.

Viticulture

Château Bauduc sits between Bordeaux's two rivers — the Garonne and the Dordogne — in the Entre-Deux-Mers, the largest wine-producing sub-region of Bordeaux. The vineyards occupy slopes (rather than the flatter plain, which is less desirable), on soils of clay-gravel with patches of clay-limestone. These mixed soils provide both the drainage that Merlot needs and the limestone structure that gives wines their mineral edge. The estate farms approximately 50 hectares of vines sustainably, with increasing moves toward organic practices. Vine age averages 25–30 years — mature enough to produce concentrated fruit, young enough to maintain vigour.

The Blend: What Each Grape Brings

Merlot (~70%) — Bordeaux's most planted variety (66% of total regional plantings, ~112,000 ha). Parentage confirmed by DNA: Cabernet Franc × Magdeleine Noire des Charentes (Boursiquot et al., 1999). Compared to Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot is thinner-skinned, lower in tannin and anthocyanin, earlier-ripening (7–10 days before Cabernet in the same terroir), and higher in sugar accumulation at lower acidity. On Bauduc's clay-gravel soils, the clay component is crucial: clay retains water, providing Merlot with the consistent moisture it requires (Merlot is more drought-sensitive than Cabernet due to shallower root systems and larger leaf area). The grape contributes the wine's dominant character: plum, red cherry, soft round tannins, supple mid-palate weight and glycerol-derived texture. In warm vintages like 2022, Merlot on clay achieves full phenolic ripeness (anthocyanin accumulation peaks earlier than in Cabernet), producing deeply coloured, immediately approachable wines.

Cabernet Sauvignon (~30%) — Parentage: Cabernet Franc × Sauvignon Blanc (confirmed by Bowers & Meredith, UC Davis, 1997). Later-ripening than Merlot by 7–14 days. Thicker skins = higher tannin concentration (2–4× more condensed tannins per berry than Merlot), higher anthocyanin, and higher concentration of methoxypyrazines (responsible for the cassis/blackcurrant/green capsicum aromatic spectrum). The gravel fraction of Bauduc's soils suits Cabernet: gravel provides warmth (reflected heat, thermal retention) and drainage, promoting full phenolic ripeness in later-ripening varieties. In the blend, Cabernet Sauvignon contributes: firmer tannic structure (longer-chain polymeric tannins), higher natural acidity (tartaric acid retention is better in later-harvested fruit), cassis/blackcurrant aromatics, and a linear architectural quality. The combination is classic Bordeaux — Merlot for approachability and flesh, Cabernet for structure, acidity and aromatic definition.

Winemaking

Native yeast fermentation in temperature-controlled tanks — no commercial yeasts, no enzymes, no adjustments. The maceration is tailored for approachability: enough skin contact for colour, flavour and fine tannin, but not so much that the wine becomes austere or demands years of cellaring. Ageing in a combination of older oak barrels and concrete (no heavy new-oak influence), preserving fruit purity and freshness. No fining, no filtration in most vintages. Bottled for early drinking without sacrificing complexity.

Tasting Notes

Intense ruby-purple colour. The nose is inviting and fresh: ripe red and black currant, dark plum, a touch of flint and subtle cedar from the brief oak contact. On the palate it is medium-bodied with supple, integrated tannins and a smooth, succulent texture — the Merlot dominance evident in its immediate approachability. Fresh acidity and a savoury, lightly mineral finish (the Cabernet's contribution) prevent it from being merely soft; there is genuine structure beneath the charm. A restaurant wine in the best sense: it works with food, it does not demand attention, and it leaves guests reaching for a second glass without thinking about it.

Vintage 2022

Gavin Quinney's own harvest report for Jancis Robinson described 2022 simply: "hot and dry, high temperatures, low rainfall." It was one of Bordeaux's warmest vintages on record — frost in spring, hail in some areas, then prolonged drought that concentrated the remaining crop. The Entre-Deux-Mers, with its clay-gravel soils, handled the drought better than pure gravel terroirs: the clay retained moisture through the driest months, buffering the vines. Yields were reduced but quality was exceptional — deeply coloured wines with powerful, ripe tannins and, against expectations for such heat, a surprising freshness. Quinney noted that producers who sorted thoroughly produced "extraordinary wines" — and Bauduc's 2022 reflects exactly that: concentration and generosity with a core of freshness that prevents heaviness.

The Producer

Nicolas Réau was meant to be a pianist. Professionally trained as a musician, his path to wine began at the age of eight when his grandfather sneaked him small glasses of red while his grandmother was not looking. That early introduction led to a parallel passion that eventually overtook the piano entirely. In 1998, he took over a 28-hectare estate, but after three years of analysis and observation, he made a radical decision: he sold 23 hectares and kept only the five best, surrounded by a medieval wall. Those five hectares became Le Clos des Treilles.

Today Réau is recognised as one of the most important natural winemakers in the Loire Valley, a regular fixture at La Dive Bouteille (France's most influential natural wine fair) and praised by Gault & Millau as producing "great Loire wines: very ripe, precise and scintillating, all vinified without sulfur or very little." He works primarily with Chenin Blanc and Cabernet Franc on clay and flint soils at the southern tip of Anjou, farming biodynamically without formal certification. His philosophy centres on purity, distinctiveness and sincerity.

Viticulture

The Coteaux de l'Aubance appellation produces sweet white wines from Chenin Blanc grown on schist and limestone soils along the Aubance river valley. The microclimate here encourages noble rot (Botrytis cinerea) in favourable years and passerillage (concentration on the vine) in others. Réau's parcels within the appellation are farmed with biodynamic principles: no synthetic inputs, cover crops, and meticulous attention to vine health and balance. The ancestral walls of the Clos provide protection and create a favourable microclimate for late-harvest conditions.

Winemaking

Hand-harvested in successive passes (tries) to select only optimally concentrated berries. Fermentation begins spontaneously with indigenous yeasts and proceeds slowly at low temperatures. The natural residual sugar is balanced by Chenin Blanc's characteristically high acidity. Little to no sulfur is added, in keeping with Réau's natural winemaking philosophy. The result is a sweet wine of extraordinary vibrancy and purity.

Tasting Notes

Aromas of candied citrus, exotic fruit, honey and white flowers. On the palate it is fresh and lively despite its sweetness, with a vibrant tension between residual sugar and bright acidity. Notes of mango, pineapple, candied yellow fruit and lime blossom develop through the mid-palate. The finish is long, gently smoky and refreshing — never heavy or cloying. This is sweet wine with energy and precision: the hallmark of great Chenin Blanc from the Loire.

Vintage 2024

The 2024 growing season in the Loire was mild and rainy, with conditions that favoured slow ripening and the development of botrytis in the Aubance valley's sheltered vineyards. For sweet wine production, these are ideal circumstances: the alternation of morning mists from the river and afternoon warmth concentrates sugars while preserving acidity. Early reports suggest 2024 is an excellent year for the Loire's sweet wine appellations, with wines of great aromatic complexity and the freshness that distinguishes the best Coteaux de l'Aubance from richer, heavier dessert wines.

Connoisseur — Matching

The Journey — We begin on a 200-million-year-old coral reef: Wittmann's Riesling from Westhofen — chalk, fossil, saline electricity. Bone-dry, 12.5% alcohol, razor-sharp. It is the purest expression of terroir transparency in this pairing: a wine that tastes like the geology it comes from. From there we cross to the southern Rhône: Beaucastel's Coudoulet Blanc is its opposite in almost every way — a four-grape blend, warm-climate, rich, textured, anise-scented. Where Riesling is linear and vertical, this is broad and layered. The contrast resets your palate's expectations entirely. Third: Kongsgaard Chardonnay from Napa — the most opulent white in the sequence. Full malolactic, barrel-aged, 14.5% alcohol. After Riesling's austerity and the Rhône's Mediterranean warmth, Kongsgaard introduces Californian power: lemon confit, baked apple, flinty salinity. Three whites, three continents, three philosophies — mineral purity, Mediterranean complexity, New World opulence. Then the turn: Ferrando's Carema Etichetta Nera — Alpine Nebbiolo, ethereal, high-acid, hauntingly delicate. After the weight of Kongsgaard, it feels like stepping from a warm room into mountain air. Rose petals, tar, wild cherry — weightless yet intense. We close with Hugel's 1999 SGN Gewürztraminer — twenty-six years old, deep gold, lychee and beeswax, lush and botrytised. From the lightest red to the richest sweet wine: a deliberate final contrast that leaves the palate satisfied rather than fatigued. The arc: crisp mineral → warm blend → opulent Chardonnay → ethereal Nebbiolo → aged sweetness.

The Producer

Two hundred million years ago, the village of Westhofen was at the bottom of a warm, shallow sea. Today, Philipp Wittmann grows Riesling on what remains of that ancient seabed: a prehistoric coral reef, dense with fossilised shells, limestone and chalk. It is this geology — not winemaking technique, not fashion — that makes Wittmann's wines taste the way they do: saline, mineral, almost oceanic. You are tasting a sea that vanished long before the dinosaurs.

Rheinhessen was for decades dismissed as Germany's bulk-wine region — an ocean of cheap Müller-Thurgau. Wittmann, along with a small group of serious estates, has demolished that reputation entirely. The domaine has been in the family for generations, farming biodynamically since 2004 (certified by Respekt, one of Europe's strictest biodynamic bodies) and holding membership of the VDP — Germany's elite quality association, the equivalent of Burgundy's finest domaines. Philipp Wittmann is now recognised as one of the country's greatest winemakers, producing Rieslings of extraordinary precision from a site that most of the wine world still underestimates.

Viticulture

The Westhofener Erste Lage is the VDP's equivalent of Premier Cru — a classified vineyard one step below Grosses Gewächs (Grand Cru). The vines root into that fossil-rich coral reef substructure, and everything about the site's character flows from that geology: the chalk provides natural drainage, the limestone gives mineral uptake, and the fossilised shells contribute a saline quality you can taste in the glass. Biodynamic preparations are used throughout the growing cycle. The continental Rheinhessen climate — warm summers, cool autumns — allows for slow, even ripening while the chalk preserves crystalline acidity even in riper years.

Winemaking

Hand-harvested and gently pressed. Fermentation begins with indigenous yeasts in a combination of stainless steel and large traditional German oak casks (Stückfass) — neutral vessels that provide slow, gentle micro-oxygenation without imposing flavour. Aged on fine lees for several months before bottling. As a Trocken wine, it is fermented completely dry: there is no residual sugar to hide behind. What you taste is pure terroir, unmasked.

Tasting Notes

This wine tastes like drinking tonic water spiked with lime over crushed chalk. It sounds unusual; it is completely addictive. Medium-bodied and beautifully balanced, with a round yet delicate palate that opens with lime zest and green tea, then deepens into stone fruit and a striking mineral saltiness — flinty, chalky, almost electric. The texture has a crunch to it, a crystalline precision that lifts the entire profile and makes you immediately want another sip. Beautifully complex without ever feeling heavy or demanding. A Riesling that rewards attention without requiring it.

Vintage 2024

Early reports on the 2024 Rheinhessen Rieslings describe wines "bursting with flavour and delineated by freshness, moderate in alcohol." After an explosive spring and fears of drought in July, autumn rains arrived at precisely the right moment, followed by a very sunny and dry September and October that allowed Riesling on the best chalk sites to achieve remarkable concentration. For Wittmann's biodynamically farmed, deep-rooted vines on the coral reef, these conditions are close to ideal.

The Producer

White wine accounts for just 5% of production in the southern Rhône. Most people associate this corner of France exclusively with red blends — Grenache, Mourvèdre, Syrah. But Beaucastel has always understood that white wine in Châteauneuf-du-Pape can be something extraordinary. Their prestige white — the Vieilles Vignes Roussanne — is considered one of the greatest white wines in France, period. Only 6,000 bottles are produced from vines at least 65 years old.

The Coudoulet Blanc is Beaucastel's way of sharing that white wine philosophy at a more accessible level. The Perrin family have farmed here since 1909 — now in their fourth generation, with brothers Marc, Thomas, Pierre and Mathieu at the helm. They were organic farming decades before anyone used the word. And the Coudoulet vineyard? It sits directly adjacent to their classified Châteauneuf-du-Pape parcels — separated only by a road and a canal. Identical terroir. Humbler appellation. The same meticulous farming. The same Perrin attention to detail. Different label, same soul.

Viticulture

The white grapes grow on the same galets roulés (smooth, heat-retaining riverbed stones) over clay and limestone that define Beaucastel's classified vineyards. These stones provide warmth for full ripeness while the mistral wind — the defining climatic factor of the southern Rhône — keeps disease pressure at zero and concentrates flavours naturally. For whites, this combination produces grapes of unusual aromatic intensity: ripe stone fruit with a mineral freshness that cooler regions cannot match. Organic farming throughout.

Winemaking

Fermentation takes place partly in oak pièces (barrels) and partly in stainless steel — a combination that builds textural complexity while preserving the freshness and aromatic purity of each variety. The wine is aged for eight months before assemblage and bottling.

The Blend: What Each Grape Brings

Bourboulenc (30%) — One of the 13 permitted white varieties in Châteauneuf-du-Pape. The latest-ripening white grape in the southern Rhône (harvest typically early-to-mid October, 2–3 weeks after Viognier). This late ripening means it retains higher malic and tartaric acid at harvest — typically 5.5–7 g/L total acidity vs 4–5 g/L for Marsanne or Viognier in the same terroir. On the galets roulés of Beaucastel, it contributes the structural backbone: citrus freshness (grapefruit, lime), mineral tension and the firm acidity that prevents the richer varieties from becoming broad or flabby. Also known as Doucillon in some southern French regions. Its natural resistance to oxidation contributes to the wine's ageing potential.

Marsanne (30%) — Northern Rhône variety (Hermitage, Saint-Joseph) also widely planted in the southern Rhône. Large-berried, early-ripening, low-acid by nature — produces full-bodied wines of 13.5–14.5% alcohol with a rich, almost oily texture. Flavour profile: white peach, quince, almond, beeswax. In the Coudoulet, Marsanne provides body and mid-palate weight — the component that gives this wine its substantial, food-friendly texture. With age (3–8 years), Marsanne develops hazelnut, lanolin and honeycomb characters. Susceptible to oxidation if mishandled, which is why Beaucastel's eight-month ageing on lees is carefully managed.

Viognier (30%) — The grape of Condrieu and Château-Grillet in the northern Rhône, now planted throughout the southern Rhône and globally. Low-acid (~3.5–5 g/L), high-alcohol potential, intensely aromatic. Key aromatic compounds: monoterpenes (linalool, geraniol) and C13-norisoprenoids (β-damascenone, β-ionone) — responsible for its characteristic peach, apricot, orange blossom and musky-spice aromas. In the Coudoulet, Viognier provides the perfumed nose: the immediate aromatic seductiveness that distinguishes this from neutral whites. Its thick skin and low natural acidity mean it contributes body and slight oiliness of texture, but no structural acidity — hence its complementarity with Bourboulenc.

Clairette (10%) — Ancient southern French variety documented since the 16th century. Fine-skinned, moderate acidity. Used as a blending component in most southern Rhône whites and historically in Clairette de Die (sparkling). Aromatic profile: white flowers (acacia), green anise, fresh herbs. In the Coudoulet, Clairette contributes finesse and aromatic lift — the subtle floral, anise-like note that appears on the finish. It is lower in alcohol potential than Marsanne or Viognier, which helps moderate the blend's overall weight. The variety is sensitive to oxidation and benefits from Beaucastel's reductive winemaking approach (partial stainless steel fermentation).

Tasting Notes

The nose is complex and inviting: layers of ripe pear, white flowers, honey and a distinctive touch of anise. On the palate it delivers wonderful texture — rich yet focused, with flavours of yellow apple, quince, white peach and almond. Good weight and presence in the mid-palate, with balanced acidity providing structure and a clean, persistent finish. This is not a neutral, refreshing white; it is a wine of substance and character, designed for food — the kind of white that guests who "only drink red" end up asking about.

Vintage 2022

The Famille Perrin described 2022 as "full of promise, with complex and aromatic palates and great balance." Across the southern Rhône, it was a warm and dry year that particularly favoured white varieties: the heat concentrated aromatics and fruit flavours, while the galets roulés' heat-buffering effect prevented the acid collapse that plagues whites in lesser terroirs during hot summers. The Perrins noted they "worked with finesse" to preserve freshness. The resulting whites show unusual richness and depth.

The Producer

John Kongsgaard is a fifth-generation Napa Valley native whose father, Thomas, was the beloved local Superior Court judge. The family's hilltop property east of the town of Napa was originally intended by John's grandfather as a stone quarry. Instead, vines were planted — and those old vines, now fifty years old, have found their equilibrium at barely one tonne per acre: miniature golden clusters from roots that have been fighting rocks for half a century. The vineyard is called "The Judge," in honour of Thomas. It is the source of Kongsgaard's most sought-after wine.

But before he made wine under his own name, John spent twenty years as winemaker for Newton Vineyards on Spring Mountain. It was there, in the early 1980s, that he produced what is widely credited as California's first unfiltered Chardonnay — a radical departure at a time when clarity and "clean" winemaking were considered paramount. That wine was spectacular, and its secret ingredient was fruit from what would later become the Judge vineyard. When John launched his own label in 1996 with wife Maggy, the philosophy was already fully formed: shy-bearing vineyards, archaic winemaking, no fining, no filtration, and an absolute faith that if you farm well enough, the winemaker's job is simply to get out of the way.

Viticulture

The estate draws from multiple vineyard sources: the family's hilltop site (volcanic soils, tiny yields) and parcels on the cooler Carneros side of Napa. The volcanic soils on the hillside produce grapes of extraordinary concentration and natural acidity — the vines, deeply rooted into rock, yield vanishingly small crops: often just one to two tonnes per acre. This is not viticulture designed for efficiency; it is viticulture designed for expression. Every cluster is hand-harvested. Production across the entire estate is tiny — perhaps 2,500 cases of Chardonnay in a generous vintage.

Winemaking

Whole-cluster pressed, fermented with indigenous yeasts in French oak barrels. Full malolactic fermentation and extended barrel ageing (14–16 months) with regular lees stirring to build texture and complexity. No fining. No filtration. Kongsgaard describes his winemaking philosophy as "archaic and low-intervention" — he uses the same Burgundian toolkit that every serious Chardonnay producer employs, but the result tastes nothing like technique. The oak is a vessel, never a flavouring agent. What you taste is vineyard and vintage, nothing more.

Tasting Notes

Antonio Galloni called the 2023 "a total stunner" — and he is not wrong. Lemon confit, white flowers, tangerine peel, baked apple tart and a light honeyed quality fill out the layers effortlessly. On the palate, it operates on a grand scale — large, opulent and layered — yet is exceptionally elegant and polished, never heavy. Poached pear, orange blossom, roasted almonds, and a flinty, almost flinty saline quality that reminds you of the finest white Burgundy. The Kongsgaard estate describes their ideal as "the flinty aroma we love in white Burgundy, and a full, layered finish focused by great acidity." That is precisely what this delivers. California Chardonnay at its absolute pinnacle — powerful, graceful, and entirely without pretence.

The Producer

Neal Rosenthal — one of America's most respected wine importers — recalls that on the very first day he imported wine, in January 1980, he visited Barbaresco in the morning and Carema in the afternoon. He has never stopped importing Ferrando since. That longevity says everything about this estate: it does not court attention, does not follow trends, and does not need to. It simply makes one of Italy's most singular wines, vintage after vintage, from a place most people have never heard of.

The Ferrando family originally came from Acqui Terme in southern Piedmont. They moved to Ivrea as wine merchants, wanting to market Piedmontese wines into the Aosta Valley. Eventually, they started making their own. The winemaking tradition dates back to 1890 (some sources say 1900), making this a family that has tended Alpine Nebbiolo for well over a century. At one point, Carema encompassed over 100 hectares of vines. The combined effects of phylloxera and the sheer difficulty of farming near-vertical terraces by hand led to a sharp decline. By the time Carema became a DOC in 1967, only 42 hectares remained. That decline continued for decades. Ferrando is the estate that held on — and is now universally recognised as Carema's crown jewel.

The Etichetta Nera (Black Label) is produced only in great vintages — never representing more than a fifth of total production. It is their selection of the finest parcels, given extended ageing. This is Nebbiolo at its most extreme and most ethereal: grown in the literal shadows of Mont Blanc, on soils that are essentially eroded Alpine rock, it produces a wine that has nothing in common with the power of Barolo but everything in common with its complexity.

Viticulture

Carema's vineyards occupy an amphitheatre of ancient terraces in the shadow of Monte Bianco. The terrain is genuinely heroic: steep, labour-intensive, farmed entirely by hand. The soils are glacial moraine — sand, granite and eroded rock — with virtually no topsoil in places. The vines are trained on the traditional "pergola canavesana," supported by stone and concrete columns called pilun. These structures serve a double purpose: protecting the grapes from the harshest mountain weather while the stone absorbs daytime warmth and radiates it back at night, compensating for the cool Alpine altitude. It is an ancient system — unchanged for centuries — and the resulting wines carry a freshness and perfume that lowland Nebbiolo simply cannot achieve. Vinified with native yeasts. Pure old-vine Nebbiolo.

Winemaking

The Etichetta Nera undergoes a minimum of three years' ageing before release — including at least two in small oak (barrique). But "small oak" at Ferrando is not about extracting vanilla or toast; it is about the slow, gentle oxidation that polishes mountain Nebbiolo's naturally high-toned, fine-boned tannins into something silky. The wine is also aged in large neutral botti. No fining, no filtration in the best vintages. The philosophy mirrors the terroir: nothing forced, nothing hurried, nothing artificial.

Tasting Notes

If Barolo is an opera — grand, dramatic, demanding patience — Carema Etichetta Nera is a string quartet: intimate, precise, and all the more affecting for its restraint. The nose is elegant and ethereal: exotic spices (cardamom, white pepper, saffron), ripe red berries, dried rose petals, and a haunting Alpine perfume — pine resin, mountain herbs, cold mineral air. On the palate, the wine is weightless yet intense, with fine-grained tannins of extraordinary delicacy, bright acidity, and flavours of wild cherry, tar and violets. The finish does not so much linger as float — it is a wine of air and light rather than density and weight. One of Italy's most singular expressions of Nebbiolo: hauntingly beautiful, impossibly fragile-seeming, yet built to age for decades.

The Producer

The 1984 wine regulations establishing the Sélection de Grains Nobles designation in Alsace are informally known as "Hugel's Law." That is not hyperbole. Jean "Johnny" Hugel — born 1924 in Riquewihr, eleventh generation of an estate founded in 1639 — spent years campaigning for the official recognition of these extraordinary botrytised wines. Before 1984, they existed without a legal framework: no minimum sugar levels, no defined harvesting requirements, no consumer guarantee of what the words on the label actually meant. Johnny drafted the law, lobbied for it, and won. The Hugels didn't simply pioneer SGN wines — they created the entire category as a legal reality.

The estate owns 30 hectares around Riquewihr, predominantly Grand Cru classified, and additionally purchases from approximately 100 hectares of contracted grower-partners. Sold in over 100 countries, Hugel remains 100% family-owned — now in its thirteenth generation. Johnny Hugel also served as president of the Alsace Grand Cru delimitation committee, personally drawing the boundaries of the classified vineyards. Few individuals have shaped an entire wine region so profoundly. He passed away in 2009, but the standards he set remain the foundation of everything the family produces.

An SGN from Hugel is something rare and almost sacred. Production is tiny and entirely vintage-dependent — if botrytis does not develop correctly, the wine is simply not made. The grapes are harvested berry by berry, well after the normal vendanges, requiring multiple passes through the vineyard over weeks. The resulting wines possess quasi-unlimited ageing potential. This 1999 is now over 26 years old.

Viticulture

The Hugel vineyards occupy prime sites around Riquewihr, sheltered by the Vosges mountains which create one of the driest microclimates in France (Colmar, nearby, is France's second-driest city). For SGN production, grapes are left on the vine deep into autumn — often November — waiting for the precise conditions that encourage botrytis cinerea: cool, misty mornings followed by warm, dry afternoons that prevent the rot from becoming destructive. The shrivelled, raisined berries are harvested in multiple passes by hand, selecting only perfectly noble-rotted fruit. Yields are vanishingly small — often just a few hectolitres per hectare. It is a gamble against weather and time; not every year delivers.

Winemaking

The concentrated must — impossibly thick with natural sugar — ferments at an agonisingly slow pace in small oak casks. Indigenous yeasts struggle against the sugar concentration and eventually surrender, arresting fermentation and leaving significant residual sweetness. This is not a decision the winemaker makes; it is a physical inevitability. No chaptalisation, no enrichment, no artifice. The concentration comes entirely from nature — from botrytis and from time on the vine. The resulting wine is aged in traditional Alsatian foudres before bottling. It will outlive most of us.

Tasting Notes

Deep, luminous gold — twenty-six years have burnished it to something regal. The nose is an opulent cascade: rose petals, lychee, cracked white pepper (the varietal Gewürztraminer signature, amplified by time), dried apricot, pineapple, honey, beeswax and a subtle resinous quality that comes only with age. On the palate, it is lush and sweet — extraordinarily rich, with a viscosity that coats the glass — yet the smooth botrytis character provides an architectural structure that prevents any sense of cloying. This is sweet wine with bones. The finish is long, spiced and mouthwatering, cutting through the sweetness with a persistent freshness. Liquid gold from the family that quite literally wrote the law defining what this wine can be.

Indulgence — Matching

The Journey — This pairing is built around escalation — each wine more concentrated, more profound, more rare than the last. We open with Zind-Humbrecht's 2005 Pinot Gris from the Rangen de Thann: volcanic terroir, twenty years of age, golden in colour, smoky and austere despite its richness. It establishes the register immediately — this is not a pairing for the faint-hearted. Second: Domaine de Montille's Puligny-Montrachet Les Folatières — Premier Cru Burgundy from the plot adjacent to Le Montrachet itself. Where the Rangen is volcanic and smoky, this is limestone and silk: white peach, hazelnut, crystalline minerality. Two aged white wines from opposing geologies — volcanic ash versus Jurassic chalk — yet both sharing precision and intellectual depth. Third: Gaja's Gaia & Rey 2016 — Italy's first great Chardonnay, from Barbaresco's limestone. After Burgundy's restraint, Gaja introduces warmth and Mediterranean confidence. Still mineral, still precise (the 2016 marks the shift to less malolactic), but with an Italian generosity that pushes the envelope wider. Then: Sine Qua Non's Eleven Confessions — California at its most uncompromising. Syrah from the Sta. Rita Hills, 15%+ alcohol, inky, brooding, concentrated. After three refined whites, this lands like a wave — dark fruit, black pepper, smoke, viscous texture. A deliberate shock that resets the palate for what follows. Fifth: Pétrus 2008 — pure Merlot on blue clay smectite. After SQN's brute power, Pétrus is velvet and precision — concentrated but controlled, powerful but elegant. The greatest Merlot in the world from a classical, structured vintage. The arc from SQN to Pétrus demonstrates two philosophies of concentration: Californian exuberance versus Bordelais restraint. We close with Château d'Yquem 2008 — one glass per vine, six-to-ten harvest passes, twenty months in new oak. The most celebrated sweet wine on earth. After five wines of escalating intensity, Yquem provides resolution: golden, honeyed, botrytised, impossibly persistent, yet carried by the acidity that prevents any sense of heaviness. The full arc: volcanic age → Burgundian silk → Italian limestone → Californian power → Bordeaux velvet → Sauternes gold.

The Producer

Olivier Humbrecht learned about biodynamics by studying manure. "When you buy compost," he has said, "it is dried out to save weight — but drying it kills all the microorganisms you want to bring to the soil. So we decided to make our own." That pragmatic radicalism — a willingness to rethink everything from first principles — is what defines Zind-Humbrecht. It is why Andrew Jefford, after his first tasting with the domaine, wrote that "my personal wine world hasn't been the same since."

The domaine was born from a marriage in 1959: Léonard Humbrecht of Gueberschwihr (a family making wine since 1620) married Geneviève Zind of Wintzenheim. Together they started with 5 hectares. Today, under their son Olivier — the first French person to earn the Master of Wine qualification — it has grown to 40 hectares across four Grand Cru sites: Rangen, Goldert, Hengst and Brand. Since 2002, Olivier has served as President of Biodyvin, the international biodynamic winemakers' association. He was also elected President of the Alsace Grand Cru Association in 2011. No one speaks for Alsace's terroirs with greater authority.

What makes this domaine extraordinary is not size or reputation, but philosophy. Every wine in a range of 30-plus is vinified separately, fermented with indigenous yeasts, and allowed to find its own natural balance between sugar and acidity — without correction. Humbrecht publishes an "indice de sucrosité" he invented himself, because his wines refuse to fit into the binary of "dry" or "sweet." They exist on their own terms. The result: a level of articulate differentiation — of terroir speaking in distinct, unmistakable voices — that is almost without parallel in France.

Viticulture

The Rangen de Thann is not merely extreme — it is almost absurd. First documented in 1272 by monks of the Abbey of Murbach, by the 17th century it was one of the most celebrated vineyards in Alsace, with 500 hectares under vine. By the 20th century, producers had largely abandoned it: the slopes are so steep (up to 90% gradient) that no machinery can operate, and the labour required is economically irrational. Today, Zind-Humbrecht is the dominant owner with 5.5 hectares, including the walled Clos Saint-Urbain parcel within the Grand Cru.

What makes the Rangen unique is its geology: volcanic. This is the only volcanic Grand Cru in Alsace — dark grey-brown sedimentary rock composed of graywacke and tuff (compressed volcanic ash), remnants of ancient eruptions. These dark rocks absorb heat during the day and radiate it back at night, creating a thermal engine that compensates for the site's late-ripening, high-altitude position at the very southern tip of the Alsace wine route. The narrow, deep valley of the river Thur channels cold winds directly across the vineyard, maintaining freshness. It is a site of violent contradictions: volcanic warmth and alpine cold, extreme gradient and maritime influence, power and precision. Nothing else in France tastes like it.

Winemaking

Grapes are harvested by hand — there is no other option on these slopes — often in late October or November. Fermentation begins with indigenous yeasts in old oak demi-muids (600-litre barrels) and traditional Alsatian foudres. Then patience: fermentation is deliberately unhurried, sometimes continuing for over a year, stopping and starting with temperature shifts, until the wine finds its own equilibrium. Humbrecht does not intervene. If the terroir produces a wine with residual sugar, he accepts it. If it ferments bone-dry, that is equally valid. The 2005 Rangen Pinot Gris is at the drier, more austere end — but you would never guess it from looking at the colour.

Tasting Notes

Dark gold in the glass — the kind of colour that makes guests assume they are about to taste something sweet. They are wrong. The nose is extraordinary: deeply smoky (that volcanic signature is unmistakable), exotic spice (ginger, saffron), waxy richness, and then a cool herbal counterpoint — menthol, chamomile, sage — that speaks to the altitude and those cold winds from the Thur. On the palate, it defies every expectation. Enormous concentration — dried apricot, beeswax, quince, smoke — yet the acidity is almost violent in its intensity, keeping everything taut, precise, electric. Despite its golden colour and opulent nose, the palate is austere and tight. This is a cerebral wine, not a hedonistic one. It demands your full attention, and if you give it, there is nothing like it anywhere in the world.

Vintage 2005

The domaine has described 2005 as "surely one of the greatest and most complete vintages we have ever produced." The growing season delivered exceptional homogeneity across grape varieties and terroirs — a rare occurrence in Alsace, where the diversity of sites typically creates wide variation. For the Rangen specifically, the combination of warmth (for full phenolic ripeness), altitude (for acidity preservation), and clean, healthy fruit (no botrytis intervention) allowed the volcanic terroir to express itself with absolute clarity. At twenty years of age, this wine is now at its peak — but it shows no signs of fading.

The Producer

If you have seen the documentary film Mondovino — shown at Cannes in 2004, a polemic about globalisation and wine — you have met Hubert de Montille. He is the elderly Burgundian in his cellar, pouring a glass with trembling hands and pronouncing, with absolute certainty, that Burgundy should taste of Burgundy. Not of oak. Not of alcohol. Not of the winemaker. Of the place.

That conviction was Hubert's life's work. A Dijon lawyer by profession, he made wine on evenings and weekends from just 2.5 hectares in Volnay — inherited vineyards that he refused to chaptalise, refused to over-extract, and refused to dress up in new oak when the fashion demanded it. His wines were famously austere in youth, light in colour, and seemingly fragile. Then they aged for decades. He was right all along; the rest of Burgundy just took forty years to catch up.

Today Hubert's son Étienne — who joined in 1983 and took over vinification in 1990 — has expanded the estate to 37 hectares across the finest Premier and Grand Cru sites of the Côte de Beaune: Volnay, Pommard, Corton, Puligny-Montrachet, Meursault. He converted to organic in 1995 and biodynamic in 2005 (certified 2012), and in 2012 acquired the Château de Puligny-Montrachet itself. The philosophy has not changed: classical structure, minimal new oak, absolute fidelity to terroir. These are wines that ask you to wait, and reward you for it.

Viticulture

Les Folatières is where Puligny-Montrachet touches greatness. Situated mid-slope, directly adjacent to Le Montrachet Grand Cru, it shares the same Bathonian limestone bedrock — the same geological stratum that produces the most celebrated white wine on earth. The vineyard faces south-southeast at an elevation that catches cooling breezes from the Hautes-Côtes, and the soils are predominantly white limestone and marl over fractured rock, providing exceptional drainage and mineral uptake. Wines from Les Folatières frequently carry a crystalline precision and stony depth that blur the line between Premier Cru and Grand Cru — and at Montille, where the winemaking deliberately refuses to flatter or amplify, that terroir signature comes through with unusual clarity.

Winemaking

Hand-harvested, whole-cluster pressed, settled, then fermented in barrel with indigenous yeasts. Oak is from the Damy cooperage — approximately 20% new — a deliberately restrained proportion. Hubert always said that new oak should never be tasted in the wine; Étienne holds the same line. The wine rests on fine lees for 14 months with minimal stirring, then is bottled typically without fining. The intention is transparency: nothing between the terroir and the glass.

Tasting Notes

This is the kind of wine that makes you lean in rather than lean back. It does not shout; it whispers. White peach, citrus blossom, crushed limestone and a subtle hazelnut quality on the nose — the oak invisible, seamlessly woven into the fabric. The palate is silky and almost weightless, yet there is real concentration beneath that elegance: it fans out gently towards the finish, unfurling layer by layer, with a mineral sapidity and stony persistence that speaks directly of its position next to Le Montrachet. A fine Folatières in the making — understated now, built to reveal itself slowly over twenty years. Classical Burgundy at its most refined.

Vintage 2022

2022 was warm and generous across Burgundy — the sort of vintage that worries purists. But on limestone, with biodynamic farming and deep root systems, the results have been remarkably fresh. The Montille team found that their terroir provided a natural buffer: the chalky subsoil maintaining acidity and mineral tension even as sugar levels rose elsewhere. The resulting white wines are generous in their fruit — riper, more immediately appealing than cooler years — yet balanced by the natural vivacity that great limestone always provides. Drinking window: 2026–2042.

The Producer

In 1978, Angelo Gaja ripped out a vineyard of Nebbiolo in Barbaresco and planted Cabernet Sauvignon. His father Giovanni, watching the old vines being uprooted, muttered "Darmagi" — Piemontese dialect for "what a shame." Angelo named the resulting wine Darmagi, turning his father's despair into a label. A year later, in 1979, he planted Chardonnay. That was worse. In the heartland of Italy's noblest red grape, planting a French white variety was not merely unconventional — it was considered a betrayal.

The first Gaia & Rey was released in 1983. It was Italy's first great Chardonnay, the first Italian white to undergo malolactic fermentation in barrique, and the first to prove that Piedmont's calcareous soils could produce white wine of genuine world-class distinction. Every winemaker in the Langhe who makes Chardonnay today is working in the space Angelo Gaja created. The wine is named after his daughter Gaia and his grandmother Clotilde Rey — two women whose influence shaped everything he built.

The Gaja story began in 1859, when Giovanni Gaja opened a restaurant in Barbaresco, making wine for his tables. Five generations later, the family farms 100 hectares across Barbaresco, Barolo, Bolgheri and Montalcino. Angelo took over in 1961 and proceeded to break every rule: green harvesting (the old farmers thought he was throwing money away), French barriques (heresy in a region of large Slavonian botti), single-vineyard designations, international varieties. Each decision was controversial. Each proved visionary. Hugh Johnson wrote that he "has the highest profile of any grower in Piedmont, aggressively taking his own line on techniques, grape varieties, style and price." Now in his eighties, with daughter Gaia increasingly at the helm, the estate continues to set standards for Italian fine wine.

Viticulture

The Chardonnay vineyard for Gaia & Rey sits in the Treiso commune of Barbaresco, planted in 1979 at approximately 460 metres elevation. The soils are calcareous marl and limestone — the same geology that produces Italy's greatest Nebbiolo just metres away. South-southwest exposure provides warmth for ripening; altitude provides freshness and temperature drop at night. This terroir imparts the distinctive salty, mineral nuances that separate Gaia & Rey from every other prestige Chardonnay: you taste the limestone's signature in every glass. Viticulture is meticulous — low yields from rigorous green harvesting, sustainable farming, obsessive canopy management.

Winemaking

The 2016 marks a significant evolution. For decades, Gaia & Rey underwent 60–70% malolactic fermentation in barrel, giving it richness and weight. With this vintage, Gaja reduced malolactic to approximately 20%. The result is a deliberate recalibration: brighter, more forward acidity, greater energy and mineral tension, less of the creamy opulence that characterised older vintages. Fermentation and ageing in French oak barriques (approximately 30% new) for six months. It is a bold choice — a winery at the very pinnacle of success choosing to evolve rather than rest on legacy. The new Gaia & Rey is sharper, more tonic, more focused. Gaja wants you to taste the limestone, not the winemaking.

Tasting Notes

Immediately more electric than older vintages. The nose is precise: lemon, grapefruit, honeydew melon, almond, delicate white flowers. No butter, no vanilla — just fruit and stone. On the palate, it is focused and linear, with a firm grip and a mineral persistence that outlasts many Chardonnays of far greater weight. The salty, almost iodine-like quality from the limestone soils is unmistakable. The finish is clean and energetic, tonic and refreshing, demanding another sip. This is not California Chardonnay, not even Burgundy — it is something entirely its own. Italy's answer to great white wine, built on the conviction that terroir matters more than technique.

Vintage 2016

A classic Piedmont vintage of balance and elegance — cooler and more measured than the immediately preceding years. Even conditions without extremes allowed slow, complete ripening and natural acidity preservation. Robert Parker's Wine Advocate awarded 94 points, specifically noting the wine's "sharp and tonic" character. For Chardonnay in Piedmont, where heat can push alcohol and strip freshness, 2016 provided ideal conditions: enough warmth for phenolic maturity without the excess that flattens personality.

Viticulture

The Sta. Rita Hills' unique east-west valley orientation channels cool Pacific fog and maritime winds directly along the vineyards. Production is tiny: a few hundred cases at most for each cuvée.

The Producer

Pétrus is the most famous wine estate on earth, yet it operates with an almost monastic simplicity. There is no château — just a modest house and a small cellar. There is no classification — Pomerol never created one. There is no second wine — if a barrel does not meet the standard, it is sold off in bulk. And there are just 11.4 hectares on the highest point of the Pomerol plateau, planted entirely to Merlot since 2010.

The estate's origins trace back to the mid-1750s, with the Arnaud family taking ownership in 1770. But Pétrus's modern legend begins with Madame Loubat, who acquired the property around World War I and introduced it to the post-war collector market. Upon her death in 1961, the estate was sold to Jean-Pierre Moueix, whose family remains the custodians today. Jean-Claude Berrouet served as cellar master from 1963 until his retirement in 2008 — the very vintage we are serving — when his son Olivier took over. That 45-year continuum of a single winemaker's vision is almost unprecedented at this level.

What makes Pétrus, Pétrus? The answer is beneath your feet: a unique "button" of dark blue clay — smectite — that exists nowhere else in Pomerol. The topsoil is 60–80 centimetres of dense clay and sand in a 90:10 ratio; beneath it lies a subsoil of incredibly dense, dark blue clay so hard that roots cannot penetrate it. This clay swells when wet, absorbing water like a sponge, then cracks when dry, naturally regulating moisture supply to the vines throughout the growing season. Blue clay produces grapes with the highest level of tannin in Pomerol while simultaneously being among the softest in texture — the paradox that defines Pétrus.

Viticulture

The vineyard sits on the absolute apex of the Pomerol plateau at approximately 40 metres elevation — modest by most standards, but the highest point in this famously flat appellation. The average vine age exceeds 40 years, with some parcels significantly older. Meticulous canopy management and rigorous green harvesting ensure only perfectly healthy, concentrated fruit reaches the winery. The Moueix family's approach is one of obsessive attention to detail in the vineyard rather than technical intervention in the cellar. Harvest decisions are made on a parcel-by-parcel basis, with picking sometimes completed in a single afternoon when conditions are right.

Winemaking

Vinification is remarkably traditional and deliberately simple: fermentation in concrete vats at controlled temperatures, followed by ageing in 100% new French oak barrels (predominantly from the Seguin Moreau cooperage) for approximately 18–20 months. There is no pump-over — only gentle punch-downs. The simplicity of the process is a statement of confidence in the raw material: when your terroir produces fruit of this quality, the winemaker's role is stewardship, not transformation. The wine is egg-white fined but not filtered before bottling.

Tasting Notes

The 2008 is possibly the most concentrated wine of the vintage across all of Bordeaux. Dark purple colour. A sweet, complex perfume of mocha, caramel, black cherries, black currants, earth and forest floor. On the palate, it is gorgeous and smooth — finely balanced with impressive tension between sweet ripe fruit, bright acidity and fine-grained tannins. The characteristic Pétrus velvet texture is in full evidence, with layers of dark plum, truffle, mineral and subtle black tea unfolding across the palate. It combines poise and power: a wine that is more about precision than weight, more about persistence than volume. The finish carries the unmistakable mineral signature of the blue clay — a stony, iron-tinged depth that no other wine in the world quite replicates. A Pétrus of finesse and intellectual beauty, now entering a magnificent drinking window.

Vintage 2008

2008 was a vintage that rewarded patience and precision in Bordeaux. After a difficult growing season — cool, damp conditions through much of the summer — September and October delivered the salvation that the Right Bank needed. The clay soils of Pomerol performed brilliantly, retaining warmth and providing the buffered moisture regulation that allowed late-ripening Merlot to reach full maturity without the stress of drought. While the Left Bank's Cabernet Sauvignon sometimes struggled to achieve phenolic ripeness, Pétrus's Merlot on blue clay thrived. The resulting wine is classical rather than opulent: elegant, structured and built for the long haul, with critics noting its extraordinary concentration despite the vintage's modest reputation.

The Producer

Château d'Yquem occupies a category of one. The only Premier Cru Supérieur in the entire 1855 Classification of Bordeaux — a rank above every other estate, including the First Growths of the Médoc. For over four centuries, Yquem has been producing what many consider the greatest sweet wine on earth, from 113 hectares of immaculately maintained vineyards on the highest point of the Sauternes appellation. The estate's elevated position, surrounded by the converging waters of the Ciron and Garonne rivers, creates the precise microclimate that botrytis cinerea demands: cool morning mists rise from the river to settle over the vines, encouraging noble rot, while warm afternoon sunshine halts the process before it becomes destructive.

The commitment to quality at Yquem is almost absurdly rigorous. Trained harvesters make between six and ten passes through the vineyard over several weeks, selecting only perfectly botrytised berries — not bunches, but individual berries. In 1997, the harvest required seven separate passes over 32 days. In lesser years, the wine is simply not produced at all: Yquem skipped 1992, 2012 and several other vintages entirely rather than compromise its name. The result of this fanaticism is a yield of roughly one glass of wine per vine — a statistic that sounds impossible until you understand the concentration that botrytis demands. The estate has been owned by LVMH since 1996 but continues under the same uncompromising standards set by centuries of private ownership.

Viticulture

The vineyards sit atop a gentle hill of gravel over clay at approximately 80 metres elevation — the highest point in Sauternes. The convergence of the cold Ciron river with the warmer Garonne creates the temperature differential that generates autumn mists essential for botrytis. Sémillon dominates the plantings (approximately 75%) because its thin skin makes it particularly susceptible to noble rot; Sauvignon Blanc (approximately 25%) provides acidity and aromatic lift to the blend. The grape variety composition in the 1642 was changed entirely to white wine grapes by the Sauvage family, establishing the template that persists today. Vine age averages 25–30 years, though some parcels are far older. No production compromises are made — if a parcel does not achieve the required concentration, it is excluded.

Winemaking

The concentrated must is fermented in 100% new French oak barrels — Yquem uses only the finest cooperages — where the wine remains for approximately 20 months. The combination of extreme natural sugar (often exceeding 20° potential alcohol), naturally preserved acidity and new oak creates a wine of extraordinary structural architecture: sweet but never cloying, rich but never heavy, powerful but never crude. Each barrel is tasted repeatedly during ageing, and only those deemed worthy of the Grand Vin are blended into the final assemblage. Rejected barrels are either declassified or, in more extreme cases, destroyed entirely.

Tasting Notes

Medium lemon-gold colour with amber reflections. The nose is forward and already complex at eighteen years of age: a compellingly savoury and earthy bouquet of toasted almonds, cloves, damp soil and crushed stones, with a core of peach preserves, lemon tart, dried pineapple and allspice. On the palate, the wine is intensely sweet yet the texture is remarkably light and smooth — silky rather than ponderous. Mandarin, ripe pineapple, liquid honey, dried apricot and saffron layer through the mid-palate, underscored by great minerality and an electric, racy acidity that prevents any sense of heaviness. The finish extends seemingly without end — wave after wave of botrytis-spiced dried fruit, honeyed citrus and that characteristic linden blossom quality that marks the greatest Yquems. A gentle, graceful, racily refined vintage.

Vintage 2008

A year of capricious weather that proved ideal for Sauternes even as it challenged red wine producers. A cold, dry period arrived in September, causing slow ripening in Sémillon conducive to great aromatic purity. The botrytis of late September and early October was of exceptionally high quality — noble rot developing evenly and cleanly. Harvesting continued well into a sunny November as producers waited for each parcel to reach its optimal state; Château Rieussec, for reference, finished picking on 19 November, the latest in living memory. Wine Enthusiast called it "a great Sauternes year" and awarded barrel samples of Yquem 97 points, praising the "intense, concentrated botrytis" and "delicious lift" of acidity. The same cold, humid conditions that produced brilliant 2008 Champagne gave Sauternes precisely what it needed: controlled moisture for botrytis without the warmth that would accelerate destructive grey rot.

Lunch — Matching

The Journey — Lunch is about pleasure without exhaustion — wines that complement without overwhelming, that satisfy without demanding. We begin with Umathum's Rosa: a saignée rosé from three Austrian varieties — Zweigelt, Sankt Laurent, Blaufränkisch — deeper and more structured than typical rosé, with genuine vinous character. It sets the tone: serious but refreshing, colourful but dry. A perfect aperitif-to-starter transition. Second: Château de Beauregard's Pouilly-Fuissé — seventeen generations of Burgundian Chardonnay on Jurassic limestone beneath the Solutré rock. After the rosé's bright fruit, the Chardonnay introduces richness and texture: white peach, toasted almond, chalky mineral finish. Warmer, broader, more substantial — the wine that carries the main course. Third: Cal Batlet's Escanya Vella from Priorat — old-vine Cariñena and Garnacha on llicorella slate. The shift from white to red: concentrated, mineral, dark-fruited, with the structural tannin to stand up to grilled meats and aged cheeses. After Burgundy's roundness, the Priorat's angular slate minerality creates a savoury contrast — darker, more austere, more gripping. We close gently: Nicolas Réau's Coteaux de l'Aubance — sweet Chenin Blanc from the Loire, light in alcohol (~11%), lively with acidity. After the Priorat's tannin and darkness, this arrives like a palate-cleanser in dessert form: quince, beeswax, honey, lifted and refreshing rather than heavy. The lunch ends lighter than it began in terms of palate weight — rosé freshness → white richness → red structure → sweet lightness. A pairing designed to leave you energised rather than fatigued.

The Producer

Josef "Pepi" Umathum does not certify his wines as biodynamic. He never has. He farms biodynamically — has done since 2005 — but refuses to put it on the label because, in his words, biodynamics is not a marketing tool. It is simply how he farms. That quiet conviction is the key to understanding everything about this estate: nothing is done for show; everything is done because it is right.

The Umathum story begins modestly. Elisabeth and Johann Umathum founded a mixed farm in Frauenkirchen in 1958 — sugar beets, grain and four hectares of vines on the eastern shore of the Neusiedlersee. Their son Josef took over in 1985 and transformed what was essentially a side project into one of Austria's most respected wine estates. He was among the first in Burgenland to practise sélection massale — propagating vines from cuttings of the entire existing vineyard rather than planting commercial clones — a technique he adopted in the late 1980s, decades before it became fashionable.

Today the estate farms 48 hectares across three distinct sites. The home vineyards in Frauenkirchen lie on the warm, flat Pannonian plain east of the lake — gravel and loam soils that produce generous, fruit-forward reds. But Pepi's most remarkable project sits across the water in Jois, where he has painstakingly restored ancient terraced vineyards first documented in 1214 as property of the Hungarian royal house. These volcanic stone-walled terraces — unique in Burgenland, almost forgotten for generations — now grow Pinot Noir and Blaufränkisch of extraordinary mineral precision. Every grape across the entire estate is harvested by hand.

Viticulture

The grapes for Rosa come from the estate's biodynamically farmed vineyards around the Neusiedlersee — a shallow, warm lake whose thermal mass creates the Pannonian microclimate that defines Burgenland: hot summers, mild autumns, and the warm winds that allow grapes to ripen fully while retaining freshness. The blend combines Austria's three principal red varieties. Vines are up to 45 years old, rooted in gravel and schist soils that bring a complex mineral undertone to the fruit. Biodynamic preparations are applied according to the lunar calendar; no synthetic inputs are used anywhere on the estate.

The Blend: What Each Grape Brings

Zweigelt — Austria's most planted red variety (~6,300 ha, ~14% of total vineyard area). Created in 1922 at the Höhere Bundeslehranstalt für Wein- und Obstbau in Klosterneuburg by Friedrich Zweigelt from a crossing of Sankt Laurent (♀) × Blaufränkisch (♂). Originally named Rotburger. Early-ripening, moderate acidity, low tannin. Produces cherry, plum and raspberry fruit with a soft, juicy mid-palate. In the Rosa, Zweigelt provides the accessible, fresh red-fruit character and the easy-drinking generosity. Its thin skin extracts colour quickly during saignée maceration — even a few hours of skin contact gives vivid salmon-fuchsia hues.

Sankt Laurent — DNA analysis confirms it is an offspring of Pinot Noir, with the second parent possibly Savagnin (Régner et al., University of Natural Resources Vienna). Named after the feast of St Lawrence (10 August), when its grapes begin to colour. Compared to Pinot Noir, Sankt Laurent is darker-skinned, higher in tannin and anthocyanin, more deeply pigmented, and ripens slightly earlier. In the Rosa, it contributes depth of colour, darker berry fruit (blackberry, blueberry), a peppery spice character and the fine tannic structure on the finish — the element that elevates this rosé above simple fruit-juice wines.

Blaufränkisch — Austria's premier red quality grape. Synonyms: Lemberger (Germany), Kékfrankos (Hungary), Frankovka (Czech Republic/Slovakia). DNA profiling shows parentage of Gouais Blanc (♂) × Blaue Zimmettraube (♀). Late-ripening (harvest typically mid-October in Burgenland), high natural acidity (6–8 g/L tartaric), firm tannins, deep colour. Characteristic flavour profile: dark cherry, blackberry, black pepper, earthy minerality. In the Rosa, Blaufränkisch provides the structural spine — the acidity and mineral grip that gives the wine food-pairing versatility and prevents the blend from being merely soft and fruity. On gravel and schist soils at Frauenkirchen, it contributes a savoury, iron-mineral undertone even in short-maceration rosé form.

Winemaking

Rosa is made by the saignée method — the traditional technique in which red grapes are crushed and left in contact with their skins for a few hours before fermentation. During this maceration, colour, aroma compounds and delicate tannins leach from the berry skins into the juice. The juice is then "bled off" (saigné) and fermented separately as rosé. This is fundamentally different from direct pressing (the method used for most Provençal rosés): saignée produces a wine of deeper colour, more structure and greater vinous complexity. It is, essentially, a pink version of the estate's top red wines — not a lightweight summer drink but a serious food wine with genuine substance. Fermentation and ageing in neutral vessels (no oak influence); minimal sulphur.

Tasting Notes

Fuchsia-salmon — immediately deeper than typical rosé, signalling the saignée method. The nose is aromatic and inviting: ripe raspberry, dark cherry, a hint of spice (white pepper, clove) and something faintly herbal — almost wild. On the palate it is silky and vinous, with genuine structure: this is not a wine that disappears after the first sip. There is juiciness and freshness, balanced by fine, barely perceptible tannins on the finish that give it grip and food-friendliness. Dry, elegant, refreshing — but with the body and depth to stand up to the richness of a lunch menu. A rosé for people who find most rosé boring.

Vintage 2022

Burgenland's 2022 was warm and dry — a vintage that favoured concentration and early ripening across the Neusiedlersee. The Pannonian heat was particularly pronounced, but the lake's moderating influence and the mineral-rich soils around Frauenkirchen prevented excess, keeping pH levels balanced and acidity fresh. For rosé production, these are ideal conditions: warm enough for full flavour development and physiological ripeness, dry enough to maintain pristine fruit health without disease pressure. The resulting Rosa shows a generosity and depth of colour that reflects the vintage's warmth, tempered by the crystalline freshness that Umathum's biodynamic farming consistently delivers.

The Producer

In 1407, a man named Jehan Burrier appeared as a witness in a famous trial. The trial determined the boundary between the Duchy of Burgundy and the Kingdom of France — one of those moments where a line drawn on a map changed history. Six hundred years later, his descendants are still making wine on the same slopes. That is not a typo. The Burrier family has been growing vines in the Mâconnais for seventeen generations.

Château de Beauregard sits at the base of the Roche de Solutré — one of Burgundy's most dramatic landmarks, a towering limestone cliff that rises 500 metres above the surrounding vineyards like the prow of a fossilised ship. The estate was acquired in the mid-nineteenth century and is now led by Frédéric-Marc Burrier, who returned to the family domaine in 1999 after years at Louis Jadot — one of Burgundy's finest négociant houses. He brought precision, ambition and a conviction that the Mâconnais deserves to be taken as seriously as the Côte d'Or. When Pouilly-Fuissé was finally granted Premier Cru status in 2020 (first vintage 2020), Frédéric was not surprised. He had been championing these terroirs for decades.

It is easy to dismiss southern Burgundy as "Burgundy light." It is also wrong. The limestone here is the same geological stratum that produces Le Montrachet — the same Jurassic bedrock, the same mineral signature. The difference is one of warmth: the Mâconnais sits further south, receives more sun, and its wines carry a generosity and immediate appeal that the Côte d'Or takes years to develop. Château de Beauregard, farming over 70 hectares across Pouilly-Fuissé, Saint-Véran and Mâcon, makes the most of that warmth while never losing sight of the limestone beneath.

Viticulture

The vineyards occupy south and southeast-facing slopes on limestone and clay soils beneath the protective shelter of the Solutré cliff. This is a warmer, more Mediterranean-influenced corner of Burgundy than the Côte d'Or, which gives the wines their characteristic ripe, generous fruit profile. But it is the limestone bedrock — and its exceptional drainage — that separates great Pouilly-Fuissé from lesser Mâcon: it provides the acidity, mineral definition and structural backbone that prevents richness from becoming heaviness. Sustainable farming throughout, with increasing moves toward organic conversion.

Winemaking

Hand-harvested grapes are whole-cluster pressed. Fermentation begins with indigenous yeasts in a combination of stainless steel tanks and older oak barrels — the proportion varies by cuvée, but the oak is always neutral, a vessel rather than a flavouring agent. Aged on fine lees for 10–12 months, building textural complexity without masking terroir. Frédéric's years at Jadot taught him a fundamental truth about the Mâconnais: the best wines here are made by getting out of the way. The terroir does the work; the winemaker's job is not to interfere.

Tasting Notes

Generous yet refined. Aromas of white peach, citrus blossom, toasted almond and a subtle mineral undertone that signals the limestone beneath the vines. The palate is round and textured — richer and more immediately appealing than typical Côte d'Or Chardonnay — with ripe stone fruit, a touch of honeyed richness, and bright, lively acidity that keeps everything focused. The finish is chalky, lightly saline, and carries a persistence that belies the wine's accessible positioning. This is Chardonnay that satisfies without demanding — complex enough to hold a sommelier's attention, approachable enough to make everyone at the lunch table happy.

Vintage 2022

A warm, generous vintage across southern Burgundy that produced wines of concentration and solar energy. The Mâconnais handled the heat better than many expected: clay-limestone soils retained moisture through the driest periods, and the region's vines — accustomed to warmth — coped with equanimity. The resulting Pouilly-Fuissés are rich and generous, with ripe tropical fruit notes, but the best (from limestone-dominated sites like those around Solutré) maintain a freshness and mineral energy that prevents heaviness. A vintage for immediate pleasure — drink now through 2028.

The Producer

In 1989, five friends — Álvaro Palacios, René Barbier, Carles Pastrana, Josep Lluís Pérez and Daphne Glorian — drove into the abandoned hill village of Gratallops and made a pact. They would buy land nobody wanted, farm slopes nobody else would bother with, and make wine from a region the world had forgotten. That experiment became the modern Priorat revolution. Within a decade, Gratallops was on every serious wine list in the world. It is still considered the spiritual epicentre of the DOQ.

Marc Ripoll Sans came after that pioneering generation. He founded Cal Batlet in 2000, restoring his family's ancient cellar in the heart of the village. But he brought something the "Gang of Five" did not have: a family inheritance of vineyards, some approaching a century old, that had been quietly tended through the decades when nobody was paying attention. While the revolutionaries had to plant from scratch, Marc already had gnarled, low-yielding Cariñena and Garnacha bush vines rooted deep into the famous llicorella slate — the shattered, glittering black-and-gold schist that defines Priorat's wines.

The "Vi de Vila" (Village Wine) classification is Priorat's answer to Burgundy's appellation system — an attempt to move beyond broad regional designations toward wines that express the specific character of individual villages. Escanya Vella is Cal Batlet's distillation of Gratallops itself: the village that started it all, captured in a glass.

Viticulture

Priorat's story begins 900 years ago with Carthusian monks from Provence, who arrived in the twelfth century and planted the first vines at Scala Dei. The terrain they chose is among the most dramatic and unforgiving in European viticulture: steep terraces rising from 100 to 700 metres, gradients reaching 60%, and soils of pure llicorella — fractured layers of slate and quartzite that glitter between the vines like flecks of gold and obsidian. This shattered rock forces vine roots metres deep in search of water, producing tiny berries of extraordinary mineral concentration from vanishingly low yields.

Cal Batlet's ten parcels span the villages of Gratallops and Torroja del Priorat — old-vine Cariñena (the oldest approaching 100 years) trained as low gobelet bushes, farmed entirely by hand because no machinery can navigate these slopes. The hot, dry Mediterranean climate provides reliable warmth; the vines survive on what the subsoil provides alone. Old-vine Cariñena is uniquely adapted to these austere conditions: where Garnacha gives warmth and generosity, Cariñena provides the mineral spine, the tannic architecture, the sense of place that makes Priorat unmistakable.

Winemaking

Hand-harvested from old-vine parcels. Vinification with careful, measured extraction — the philosophy is concentration and minerality, not brute force or over-extraction. Ageing in a combination of concrete and used oak allows terroir to speak without new-wood domination. Each variety contributes its character to the final blend: Cariñena provides the structural backbone (mineral, tannic, deeply complex); Garnacha contributes warmth, perfume and generous flesh; small proportions of Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah add aromatic complexity and structural reinforcement. The result is a Priorat of precision rather than power — terroir-driven, not winemaker-driven.

Tasting Notes

Dense, dark and mineral-driven. The nose offers concentrated aromas of blackberry, dark cherry, dried Mediterranean herbs (rosemary, thyme, lavender), graphite and crushed slate — you can smell the terroir before you taste it. On the palate it is medium to full-bodied with firm but polished tannins, bright acidity and a distinctive savoury, almost saline quality that lifts the dark fruit and prevents any sense of heaviness. There is a compactness and coiled energy here that distinguishes it from Priorat's more extracted, powerful blockbusters. This is a wine about precision and transparency rather than sheer weight. The slate minerality persists long on the finish, joined by hints of smoke and dried herb. At the lunch table, it has the structure to stand up to grilled meats and aged cheeses while remaining fresh enough to invite another glass.

Vintage 2023

Priorat's 2023 was characterised by a dry winter followed by moderate spring rainfall — unusual for the region and welcome for replenishing deep subsoil moisture. Summer was hot but not excessively so by Priorat standards, with nighttime temperatures dropping sufficiently to preserve acidity. The old-vine Cariñena, deeply rooted and accustomed to drought, handled the conditions with equanimity. Early reports suggest wines of balance and definition — perhaps slightly less concentrated than the scorching 2022, but with greater freshness, aromatic nuance and drinkability. A vintage that favours elegance over power.
